1 Data and Methods

1.1 Data

To answer the research questions, I make use of the Friendship and Identity in School (FIS) dataset (Leszczensky et al. 2022). The data were collected in six waves between May 2013 and March 2017 among 9,292 adolescents at ten secondary schools in the German federal state North Rhine-Westphalia. A sampling frame of schools was developed based on the share of students with Turkish or otherwise foreign citizenship in order to capture settings with ample opportunity for interethnic contact. Participants were in the 5th, 6th, or 7th grade during the first wave. add something about school transition. Students filled in the paper questionnaire in-class, resulting in student response rates of 76.3%-87.8% per wave. Among other topics, participants were asked about various types of social relationships with peers within their same grade, enabling the construction of directed grade-level socionets.

1.1.1 Friendship Networks

Directed grade-level friendship networks were constructed based on the peers each student nominated as their ‘best friends’. Students could nominate up to ten best friends from within their class or other classes within the same grade.

1.1.2 Node attributes

Group Identification

Group identification was measured using the question: “How do you see yourself?”. Participants could choose between the options only as German, more as German, both equally, more as a person from the country of origin of my family, and only as a person from the country of origin of my family. Students were classified as members of one of four groups: non-migratised majority adolescents, dual identifying adolescents, German-identifying adolescents with migrant heritage, and other-than-German-identifying adolescents. Students were marked as non-migratised majority adolescents if none of their parents and grandparents were born in a country other than Germany. These participants were not posed any further questions about ethnic/cultural self-identification. Only students who reported identifying equally as German and as a person from their family’s origin country were considered dual identifiers, in line with previous studies in the German context (Jugert, Leszczensky, and Pink 2018). Students who reported having one or more (grand-)parent born abroad, but who identified only or more as German, were considered German-identifying adolescents with migrant heritage. Finally, students who viewed themselves as only or more as a person from their family’s origin country were categorised as other-than-German-identifying adolescents. A robustness check was conducted in which students identifying as mostly German or mostly part of their family’s origin country were included in the dual identifier category. Since identity is a dynamic construct, particularly in the adolescent phase of development, group identification was treated as a time-varying attribute.

Control Variables

Information about students’ primary school, neighbourhood of residence, and specific classroom were used to control for contact opportunities that may affect the prevalence of intergroup friendship but are likely to be part of the social influence process of interest. Gender was included to control for the well-known tendency for gender homophily among adolescents.
