2 Final Paper

I also started to search for articles that might be relevant for my own final paper as well as to present in the second week. Since I was interested in the topics addressed by Leszczensky et al’s (2022) FIS data, e.g. dual identities, perceived permeability, and intergroup attitudes, I searched for more publications addressing dual identity from a social network perspective. This resulted in an interest in the potential for dual-identifiers to act as a bridge between mono-identifying peers. I found several relevant publications, of which I would like to present the following in next week’s lab session:

Jugert et al.’s (2018) study of adolescents’ social networks suggests that dual identifiers may indeed be able to fulfil a ‘bridging’ function. They find that ethnic majority students prefer friendships with dual identifying peers over friendships with mono-minority identifying classmates, to the extent that they did not differentiate between dual identifiers and fellow momo-majority students. Interestingly, mono-minority adolescents developed more friendships with mono-majority and dual identifiers than with mono-minority identifying peers. Dual identifiers themselves preferred friendships with fellow dual identifiers, minority students with strong host-country identification, and mono-majority peers over friendships with mono-minority classmates. This suggests that dual identifying adolescents are indeed a popular choice of friend for peers with different identities.

A recent study by Chen et al. (2026) investigated the friendship preferences of adolescents with various ethnic identities. They found that mono-majority identifiers did not differentiate between peers with a mono-minority and dual identity. Mono-minority identifiers on the other hand preferred peers with a same-ethnic dual identity over mono-majority identifiers. Similarly, dual identifiers preferred same-ethnic fellow dual identifiers and mono-minority peers over mono-majority peers. In contrast to Jugert et al’s (2018) findings, these results suggests that dual-identifying adolescents do not necessarily function as bridges, as they are no more likely to develop multi-ethnic social networks than their mono-identifying peers.

Finally, Zhao (2023) takes a more macro-level social network approach to studying interethnic relations. The author finds that initial network segregation predicts subsequent ethnic homophily in network evolution.

3 This leads me to the following initial research questions:

Macro: Is the share of dual-identifying students associated with decreasing levels of network segregation over time?

Micro: Are friendships between mono-identifying and dual identifying students associated with an increased likelihood of friendships between mono-identifying majority and minority students over time?
