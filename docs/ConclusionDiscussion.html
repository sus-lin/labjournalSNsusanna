1 Conclusion

This study investigated patterns of homophily based on caregiver educational attainment in a network of 121 first-year university students in a Michigan Learning Community. I made use of Stochastic Actor-Oriented Models to estimate relevant influences on selection in the presence of well-known relational effects. This method additionally allowed me to differentiate between endowment and creation effects. I posed three research questions:

  1. To what extent are hanging out networks of first-year university students in a Michigan Learning Community segregated by caregiver educational attainment?

  2. To what extent do students demonstrate a tendency for homophily in the creation and maintenance of hanging out relations?

  3. Does growing up in an educationally mixed family affect students’ tendency for homophily in the creation and maintenance of hanging out relations?

Descriptive analysis based on Moran’s I did not support the hypothesis that hanging out networks were segregated by caregiver educational attainment (H1). The expectation that students prefer hanging out with peers who have the same caregiver educational background (H2) was also not supported. Although homophily did play a role in creation, as an evaluation effect, it was insignificant. Contrary to hypothesis 2b, homophily did not mainly occur in tie maintenance/endowment. Instead, an anti-homophily effect was found in endowment that counteracted the positive effect of homophily in tie creation. Finally, the expectation that students who grew up in an educationally mixed family would have lower levels of homophily was not supported (H3). All conclusions regarding the hypotheses remained constant when accounting for ethnic and gender homophily.

2 Discussion

Firstly, this study demonstrates the value of separating creation and endowment effects. In the evaluation-only model, homophily seemed to play no role in students’ social networks. This was reflected in the described level of segregation. However, separating creation and endowment effects revealed that homophily does play an important role in the tie creation stage, which is counteracted by the anti-homophily effect in tie maintenance. This preference for peers with a different background in friendship maintenance contradicts common findings on durability of ties, which generally conclude that homophilous friendships are more stable (Velez et al. 2026; Hafen et al. 2011). It is possible that relational mechanisms such as reciprocity and closure take over after initial selection homophily in explaining such persistence. However, in this case, we would expect a lack of significant homophily effect in endowment rather than a negative effect large enough to completely counteract the initial selection-based homophily. An alternative explanation could be that first-year college students are driven by a desire to encounter new perspectives. Previous research identified a wish among first-year college students to pursue personal growth and explore their own identity through engaging with peers who have different experiences (Salusky et al. 2024). This desire may make homophilous ties disappointing, resulting in the anti-homophily effect in endowment. Furthermore, in the investigated learning community, a very large number of ties was broken between waves. The apparent instability in these students’ hanging out networks may have exacerbated the role of the anti-homophily effect for macro-level outcomes. It would therefore be interesting to explore how homophily in tie creation and endowment affects segregation in networks with a lower turnover.

Many previous studies among first-generation students discuss experiences related to income inequality, demonstrating an important overlap between educational heritage and wealth. One publication even noted that all first-generation college students participating in the study were receiving a scholarship or grant (Salusky et al. 2024). This is reflected in some of the barriers these studies describe to intergroup friendship formation, e.g. first-generation college students having demanding side jobs, or students with a heritage of college attendance going out to buy lunch rather than bringing their own (Gray et al. 2018; Salusky et al. 2024). The current research however only considered educational heritage, which is a severe limitation. It is quite possible that social segregation and homophily are mainly driven by the intersection of being a first-generation college student and coming from a household with a lower income level. This could explain the finding that homophily was more relevant in tie creation than in endowment. Visible attributes that signal social status discussed in the literature on first generation students primarily relate to purchases enabled by greater wealth (Gray et al. 2018; Salusky et al. 2024). Part of the homophily effect in creation may therefore be attributable to income homophily rather than homophily in caregiver educational attainment, which is less visible. Future studies should include both caregiver education and income to investigate whether the pattern of homophily in creation and anti-homophily in endowment persists on both attributes.

In addition to income, future studies can explore how gender and ethnicity interact with caregiver education-based homophily. The current study only included these attributes and homophily based on them as control variables that may explain part of the network dynamics regarding segregation. However, previous research has shown that not only is there important overlap between first-generation college students and students with a minoritised ethnic identity, but that classed social interactions are strongly shaped by gendered and racialised dynamics. Cross-class interactions took on a different meaning within same-ethnic contacts (Ferguson and Lareau 2021), first-generation college students with a minoritised ethnic identity faced intersectional micro-aggressions and demonstrated different coping strategies than their white peers (Gray et al. 2018), and same-ethnic or same-gender relations proved important in navigating cross-class contexts (Crozier, Reay, and Clayton 2019; Gray et al. 2018; Salusky et al. 2024). Seriously taking into account such processes requires an intersectional approach that goes beyond simply controlling for ethnic and gender homophily.

The lack of reduced homophily among students who grew up with educationally mixed parents/caregivers is puzzling. One explanation could be that there were simply not enough students from mixed families to identify an effect. Alternatively, the influence of parents/caregivers in mixed families on children’s class-related identity, attitudes and skills may be less equal than expected. Historically, class categorisation of families has often only taken into account one partner’s position (generally, the man’s), assuming that this would dominate the family dynamic (Sobel et al. 2004; Mills 1994). If this is indeed the case (whether gender-based or based on dominance of diploma-holders), one parent/caregiver may not transmit their classed experiences with their children, explaining the lack of reduced homophily. To identify whether students from educationally mixed families can act as a gateway group, further research is therefore needed on cross-class interactions within families and how and to what extent these contribute to identity complexity.
