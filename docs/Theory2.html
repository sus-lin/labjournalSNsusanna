1 Theory

1.1 Class-Based Segregation and Homophily

The people of the United States are strongly segregated based on class, in terms of both income and educational attainment (Mijs and Roe 2021). Analysis of General Social Survey data indicates that an increasing difference in the number of years of education received negatively affected the probability of naming someone as a core confidant, and that this association has strengthened between 1985 and 2004 (Smith, McPherson, and Smith-Lovin 2014). This societal segregation is likely to extend to college communities. Students whose parents have no university experience have a lower likelihood of attending college in the first place, and particularly a four-year programme (Sandefur, Meier, and Campbell 2006). The effect of at least one parent having a college diploma on enrollment is even more pronounced for selective colleges (Buchmann, Condron, and Roscigno 2010), which the Michigan College of Literature, Science, and the Arts (LSA), with its 18% admissions rate (“LSA at a Glance” 2022), can certainly be classified as. Importantly, the relevance of family background does not disappear for those who do enroll in a college programme. Studies on the experiences of first-generation college students highlight that classism (Allan, Garriott, and Keene 2016), microaggressions (ÁlvarezRivadulla et al. 2023a; Gray et al. 2018a), and insensitivity (Salusky et al. 2022) create barriers to forming and maintaining cross-class friendships. I therefore expect that the college social network will be segregated along students’ parental educational backgrounds:

H1: Students who hang out together are more similar with regards to parental educational backgrounds than students who do not hang out together.

This macro-level segregation would be explained on the micro-level by homophily, the preference for homogeneity in friendships. For first-generation college students, friendships with peers who have a similar background can provide a safe basis for social support based on shared experience (Gray et al. 2018b). By spending time with other first-generation students, they can avoid the negative, stigmatising experiences above (Allan, Garriott, and Keene 2016; ÁlvarezRivadulla et al. 2023a; Gray et al. 2017; Salusky et al. 2022).

ADD REASONS: students with generational heritage of educational experience may hold prejudice, first-generation students may seek safety/avoid stigmatisation

H2: Students prefer hanging out with peers who have the same caregiver educational background.

While previous studies suggest that outward status symbols such as clothing styles can signal class background (Gray et al. 2018b; Crozier, Reay, and Clayton 2019a), more detailed information about fellow students’ family background often only becomes known through sustained interaction. Many first-generation students, both in the US and elsewhere, actively avoid disclosure and develop passing strategies such as dodging the issue or code-switching to escape stigmatisation (Gray et al. 2018c; ÁlvarezRivadulla et al. 2023b; Crozier, Reay, and Clayton 2019b). Parental educational background can therefore be considered a less observable or interaction-revealed attribute. Such attributes primarily become relevant for homophily at the tie maintenance/dissolution stage (Grund 2026). This suggests the following nuance to hypothesis 2:

H2b: Homophily in caregiver educational background mainly manifests in the maintenance/dissolution of ties rather than the creation of ties.

1.2 Family Socialisation

Importantly however, social distance between different group categories is not fixed. As evidenced by the observed tendency for national average homophily on specific categories to fluctuate over time (Smith, McPherson, and Smith-Lovin 2014), it is a context-dependent measure. Just as context may change over time on a national level, it may differ between communities or personal networks. This study therefore considers class categorisation to be a social construct, and its relevance for behaviour as something that is socially learnt.

An important share of social learning happens within families. Values, norms, beliefs, and collective narratives are transmitted to children through daily family practices, contributing to early social identity formation among children (Amin 2023). A wide range of literature explores class-based differences in family practices, from infancy into young adulthood (Stuij 2015; Van Stee 2022; Le Moal, Michaud, and Coveney 2024; Hobson et al. 2024; Reeves 2015; Chen 2026). In many cases, these studies build on Bourdieu’s theory of habitus, practice, and capital, which posits that people obtain skills, knowledge, practices and schemes that help them navigate a specific social arena (Stuij 2015). A common critique of investigations into class-based differences in parenting practices is that they are over-deterministic and can pathologise the practices of specific groups (Crompton 2006). It is unsurprising that many such studies present a somewhat simplified picture of family composition as determined by a single, shared identity dimension.

In reality, families are complex and dynamic entities, composed of members with a wide variety of social identities that both overlap and differ. A line of theorising within intergroup scholarship therefore looks at the family as an intergroup domain, with a shared family identity as the common ingroup that connects members with varied group affiliations (Soliz 2010). Soliz’s (2010) intergroup perspective on families highlights how the family becomes a site where its members learn to navigate differences while maintaining a shared family identity. In social network terms, a process of social influence occurs between people who are tied by their family relationship and differ in individual attributes, allowing them to learn from each others’ experiences. Distinction between active and passive influence? Also, link to identity complexity (maybe before ‘in social network terms’?)

Previous research on this process has mainly focused on differences in age, racialisation/ethnicity, sexual/romantic orientation, and religion. However, the same concept can be applied to class relations. Although many families are homogeneous in class composition, there are also families where members differ in their educational background or the income levels they are used to. An earlier study has already provided empirical evidence of family members’ social class influencing individuals’ support for income redistribution (Paskov and Weisstanner 2022). This supports the notion that having one parent without university experience may benefit students’ motivation to avoid discriminating based on parental educational background in their social ties. I thus pose the following hypothesis:

H3: Students whose parents have different educational backgrounds (“mixed background”) will demonstrate lower levels of homophily in friendship maintenance than students whose parents have both attained the same level of education.
