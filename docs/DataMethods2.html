1 Data and Methods

1.1 Data

To answer the research questions, I make use of a subset of the Michigan Residential Community Survey (Rambaran 2025). Data was collected among two consequtive cohorts of first-year university students in Michigan Learning Communities (MLCs). Students filled in the survey twice: a first time in October-November and a second time in March-April. Although data was collected among ten MLCs and two control groups, for the sake of this course, my analyses will be conducted on data from one learning MLC only (n = 121). The survey included a wide range of measures related to campus life, educational experiences, cultural and community experiences, social connections, feelings, and students’ backgrounds. For the purpose of this study, I make use of questions on students’ social connections and backgrounds.

1.1.1 Hanging Out Networks

A complete directed social network (i.e. socionet) was constructed based on the peers each student reported they hung around with and talked to the most (“Which students in your MLC do you hang around with and talk to the most?”). Students could nominate up to ten peers from within their MLC. Participants were instructed to skip the question if they could not think of anyone.

The data subset contained only students who responded to the network question in at least one wave. Non-responses were marked as NA and treated as uninformative in modelling.

1.1.2 Caregiver Educational Attainment

Caregiver educational attainment was measured through two versions of the same closed question, one referring to parent/caregiver 1 and one to parent/caregiver 2: “What is the highest level of education that your parent or caregiver [1 or 2] has completed?” Students could choose between the following categories: 1) 8th grade or lower, 2) some high school, no diploma, 3) high school diploma or equivalent, 4) some college credit, no degree, 5) trade/technical/vocational training, 6) associate’s degree, 7) bachelor’s degree, 8) master’s degree, 9) professional degree (e.g. Medical Doctor, lawyer, etc.), 10) doctorate degree. These questions were asked in both waves. Answers were not always consistent between waves. For practical purposes, in these cases, I proceeded with the mean between both answers, rounded upwards. If students answered the question in one wave but not the other, the answer that was provided was used.

Highest Caregiver Educational Attainment

Studies of educational inequality in the US have indicated that having at least one parent/caregiver with a bachelor’s degree is strongly associated with likelihood of college attendance (Sandefur, Meier, and Campbell 2006; Buchmann, Condron, and Roscigno 2010). This implies a strong relevance of caregiver college experience. Students were therefore classified as first-generation students if they had no parent/caregiver who had attained at least a bachelor’s degree. Students with at least one parent/caregiver with a bachelor’s degree or more educational attainment were classified as having inherited parental resources in terms of college attainment.

Mixed Family

Students were classified as coming from a mixed family if one parent had a bachelor’s degree or more educational experience, whereas the other parent had no college diploma.

1.1.3 Gender

Gender was measured as a binary variable based on administrative data. Although this was collected in both waves, no student changed their reported gender identity between the time points. Gender was therefore treated as a constant covariate. If data was missing from one wave but not the other, students were classified as the gender reported in the non-missing wave.

1.1.4 Ethnicity

Ethnic identity was categorised in eight groups: Asian, Black, Hispanic, White, Multiracial, Hawaiian, Middle Eastern/North African, and American Indian/Native American. The investigated learning community did not include any students identifying as Middle Eastern/North African or American Indian/Native American. As with previous variables, if data was missing from one wave but not the other, students were classified as the ethnic identity reported in the non-missing wave.

1.2 Analytical Methods

I make use of Stochastic Actor-Oriented Models (SAOMs) in RSiena, using R version 4.5.1 of R Statistical Software (Snijders et al., n.d.; R Core Team 2025). SAOMs takes an actor-based approach by modelling the individual network choices (‘ministeps’) made by nodes in the network. For each ministep, the probability of each possibility (making a tie, breaking a tie, maintaining the current situation) is determined based on a set of effects provided by the researcher. The model simulates the network evolution a large number of times, refining estimates for each included effect at every iteration. Model-building was conducted using an iterative approach driven by both data and theory. First, an initial theory-based model was run for each hypothesis, see descriptions below. In case of persistent non-convergence, the model was simplified. Once a model converged, goodness of fit was assessed based on several macro-level network descriptives: in-degree distribution, out-degree distribution, geodesic distribution, and a triad census. Models were refined by adding potentially relevant relational effects or removing non-significant effects until fit was deemed appropriate.

I defined three initial models.

The first model tests the endowment and creation effects of homophily based on caregiver educational attainment in the presence of well-known relational effects. To start, the relational effects included in the model were an outdegree (density) effect, reciprocity, GWESP (used to account for transitivity), indegree - popularity, outdegree - activity, and indegree - activity. As I am not interested in differentiating between creation and endowment for these effects, they were included as evaluation effects. To test the hypothesis, the model included homophily based on caregiver educational attainment as both an endowment and a creation effect. To this end, the lower-order components of homophily (ego’s characteristic and alter’s characteristic) were also included as both endowment and creation effects.

The second model tests whether coming from a mixed family changes the reduces the homophily effect. In addition to the model 1 effects, model 2 therefore initially includes ego’s mixed background characteristic (yes/no, as both endowment and creation effect), and the interaction of this effect with homophily (again, both as endowment and as creation).

In a third and final model, all effects are tested in the presence of control variables: ego’s gender, alter’s gender, gender homophily, ego’s ethnic identity, alter’s ethnic identity, and ethnic homophily.
