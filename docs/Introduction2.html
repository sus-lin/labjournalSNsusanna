1 Introduction

College is an important site for developing social ties that can benefit students throughout their lives. Social networks developed during education have been shown to play a large role in for example investment and employment decisions (Josefy, Harrison, and Howard 2023; Cohen, Frazzini, and Malloy 2008). However, not all students benefit equally from this ‘old boys’ network. Underrepresented students, such as first-generation or low-income college students, do not necessarily experience social advancement from attending a selective college (Friend 2021). First-generation college students have also repeatedly been found to have a higher probability of dropping out than their continuing-generation peers with at least one parent who attended college (Weisen et al. 2024; Pratt et al. 2019). This high attrition has been partly attributed to a lack of social integration (Rubin 2012; Dika and D’Amico 2016).

In short, first-generation college students seem to face barriers in the development of social ties with peers, particularly with peers who provide social capital. This can result in persisting inequalities. It is therefore crucial to gain a better understanding of how ties between first-generation college students and their continuing-generation peers evolve. Knowing the mechanisms through which segregation takes place can help inform interventions. For example, if segregation evolves due to homophily in tie creation but not maintenance, intervention can aim to increase meeting opportunities between first- and continuing-generation students. Alternatively, if segregation evolves due to homophily in tie maintenance rather than tie creation, efforts may be better spent on strengthening existing intergroup relations.

Although previous studies on first-generation college students have repeatedly emphasised the importance of social relations, a true social network perspective, which acknowledges the interdependency between individuals, is missing. This not only paints a less reliable picture of network development due to interdependencies that interfere with traditional statistical techniques, but also misses out on identifying the exact mechanisms behind tie creation. I therefore use stochastic actor-oriented models to answer the following questions:

RQ1: To what extent are hanging out networks of first-year university students in a Michigan Learning Community segregated by caregiver educational attainment?

RQ2: To what extent do students demonstrate a tendency for caregiver education-based homophily in the creation and maintenance of hanging out relations?

Additionally, I add to the existing literature on classed social dynamics in first-year college students by introducing a second generational factor: having a mixed inheritance, e.g. growing up with one parent/caregiver who has a college diploma and one who does not. Scholars have long been calling for the acknowledgement of class heterogeneous families (Graetz 1991). In the literature on intergroup relations specifically, the existence of these families suggests the possibility of a gateway group, defined as “groups characterized by unique social categorizations that enables them to be categorized as and identified with more than one group within the context of intergroup relations” (Levy et al. 2017). If young people who grew up in an educationally mixed family indeed feel affiliated with both their parents’/caregivers’ backgrounds, they may act as a mediator between their fellow continuing-generation students and first-generation peers. To provide a preliminary understanding of the potential for students from educationally mixed families to act as a gateway group, I therefore also investigate the following:

RQ3: Do students who grew up in an educationally mixed family demonstrate a different level of caregiver education-based homophily in the creation and maintenance of hanging out relations?
