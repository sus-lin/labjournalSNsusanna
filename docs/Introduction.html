1 Introduction

All accross Europe, ethnonationalist narratives are gaining traction. The past three decades have seen a marked increase in the popularity of populist far-right parties that draw on the notion of an ethnoculturally homogeneous nation-state to present specific out-groups as a threat to the nation (Aktas 2024; Svraka 2024). In Germany, the rise of the radical right is symbolised by the electoral victories of Alternative für Deutschland (AfD), the most recent of which concerns the state election in Sachsen-Anhalt, where the party gained a staggering 43.8% of votes (Kirby and Parker 2026). The AfD presents immigrant ‘Others’ as threats to the German people based on an essentialised understanding of their cultural identities as rigid and incompatible with ‘German’ values and civilisation (Grippo 2026). The party is highly popular among the youngest voters, indicating that such ethnonationalist attitudes are prevalent across generations (Heinze and Scheuer 2026).

Many youth however, by the very way they define their own identities, challenge such essentialised and reductive understandings of cultural identity. Holding multiple identities, also referred to as dual or bicultural identification, refers to the negotiation between and integration of different group memberships in one’s sense of self (Verkuyten et al. 2019). By identifying as part of both the country they reside in and their parents’ heritage countries, dual identifiers demonstrate that various cultural identities are not mutually exclusive but can overlap even within one person. Scholars have wondered whether dual identifiers’ connection to different identity categories can boost intergroup relations (Levy et al. 2017). However, most of the research on social networks with dual identifiers has focused on the prevalence of dual identifying youth’s friendships with differently identifying peers, rather than the influence dual identifiers exert on those they form ties with (see e.g. (Chen et al. 2026; Jugert, Leszczensky, and Pink 2018; Leszczensky et al. 2016; Leszczensky 2018)). Results of these studies are inconsistent. Some find that dual identifying adolescents are indeed a popular choice of friend for various types of mono-identifying peers (Jugert, Leszczensky, and Pink 2018; Leszczensky et al. 2016; Leszczensky 2018), suggesting that dual identifiers may function as structural bridges between different peer groups. Others see no evidence to suggest that dual identifiers are more likely to develop ethnically diverse friendship networks than mono-identifying peers (Chen et al. 2026).

However, even if dual identifying adolescents do indeed form a bridge between differently identifying peer groups, the effect of this position remains unknown. Do dual identifiers influence the homophily tendency of mono-identifying friends? In this study, I take a longitudinal social network approach to investigate the influence dual identifying adolescents exert on their friends’ friendship selection. To do so, I ask the following questions:

Do dual identifying adolescents in German secondary schools have a higher betweenness centrality than mono-identifying peers? –> needs to be workshopped. Do networks with a larger share of dual identifiers become less segregated over time?

To what extent is majority adolescents’ tendency for ethnic homophily in friendship selection influenced by having dual-identifying friends?

This study focuses on the development of social networks in the first years of secondary school. Research into ethnic identity development and homophily in late childhood/early adolescence is scarce. However, it has been suggested that salience of ethnic identity increases accross school transitions among minoritised groups (French et al. 2006). In a study among German children with Turkish heritage, two classes were identified - one pertaining children who held and retained a high level of Turkish identification, and one pertaining children whose Turkish identification increased across the transition to secondary school (Spiegler et al. 2018). Additionally, the transition is a turbulent stage in children’s friendship networks. In many cases, primary school friendships are ruptured as children seek to build new relationships in an unfamiliar social environment (Ng-Knight et al. 2019; Pratt and George 2005; Weller 2007). At the same time, supportive relationships with school friends intensify (Cantin and Boivin 2004), which especially girls increasingly value (Rudolph and Dodson 2022). It is therefore pertinent to gain more knowledge on the development of interethnic social ties in the first years of secondary school.
